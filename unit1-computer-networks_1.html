Unit I — Introduction · Physical Layer · Data-Link Layer

The wire, the signal, and the first two layers.

Everything in this unit answers one question: how do raw bits get from one machine to a directly connected neighbour, correctly? Master the models (OSI, TCP/IP), the physics (signals, impairment, capacity limits), and the first real protocols (line coding, ARP, CRC, CSMA/CD) and the rest of the course is layering on top.

§ 01Data Communications

Definition Data communication is the exchange of data between two devices via some transmission medium. For it to work, the communicating devices must be part of a system made of hardware and software.

The five components

  1. Message — the information (text, numbers, images, audio, video).
  2. Sender — device that sends the message (computer, phone, camera).
  3. Receiver — device that receives it.
  4. Transmission medium — physical path (twisted-pair, coax, fiber, radio waves).
  5. Protocol — the set of rules governing communication; without it, devices may be connected but cannot communicate (like two people speaking different languages).

Effectiveness depends on four characteristics

  • Delivery — data reaches the correct destination.
  • Accuracy — data is delivered without alteration.
  • Timeliness — delivered in time (crucial for audio/video: "real-time" delivery).
  • Jitter — variation in packet arrival time must be low.

Data flow (direction of transmission)

ModeBehaviourExample
SimplexOne direction only; one device transmits, the other only receives. Entire channel capacity used one way.Keyboard → CPU, TV broadcast
Half-duplexBoth can transmit, but not at the same time; channel capacity switches direction.Walkie-talkie, CB radio
Full-duplexBoth transmit simultaneously — either the link has two physical paths or capacity is divided.Telephone call

§ 02Networks and Network Types

Definition A network is the interconnection of a set of devices (hosts/end systems and connecting devices like routers and switches) capable of communication, linked by wired or wireless transmission media.

Network criteria

  • Performance — measured by transit time and response time; depends on number of users, medium type, hardware, software. Evaluated via throughput and delay (often a trade-off).
  • Reliability — frequency of failure, recovery time, robustness in catastrophe.
  • Security — protecting data from unauthorized access and damage; recovery policies.

Physical topology & connection type

  • Point-to-point — dedicated link between two devices.
  • Multipoint (multidrop) — more than two devices share one link (spatially or time-shared).

LAN — Local Area Network

  • Privately owned; connects hosts in a single office, building, or campus.
  • Each host has an address that uniquely identifies it in that LAN.
  • Early LANs used a common cable (bus) — every packet was seen by all hosts; today a switch forwards each packet only to its destination, allowing simultaneous pairs of hosts to communicate.

WAN — Wide Area Network

  • Wider geographic span (town, state, country, world); normally created and run by communication companies, leased by organisations.
  • Interconnects switches/routers rather than end hosts directly.
  • Point-to-point WAN: two media-connecting devices joined by a line. Switched WAN: network of point-to-point WANs joined by switches (backbone of the global Internet).

Internetwork (internet) vs the Internet

When two or more networks are connected via routers, the result is an internetwork (internet, small "i"). The global Internet (capital I) is the largest internetwork: a hierarchy of backbones (international ISPs), provider networks (regional/national ISPs), and customer networks at the edge, connected at peering points.

§ 04Network Devices: Modem, Hub, Switch, Router

Modem (modulator–demodulator)

Converts digital data from a computer into an analog signal suitable for an analog medium (e.g., telephone local loop) and back again at the far end. Modulates on transmit, demodulates on receive. DSL and cable modems are the modern descendants.

Hub

A physical-layer device — a multiport repeater. It regenerates the bit stream received on one port and blindly sends it out every other port. No addresses, no filtering, no intelligence.

  • All connected hosts share one collision domain and one broadcast domain; effectively half-duplex; bandwidth is shared.

Switch

A data-link-layer device. It learns which MAC address lives on which port (by reading source addresses of incoming frames, building a switching/MAC table) and forwards each frame only to the port of its destination.

  • Filtering: frame goes out one port, not all.
  • Each port is its own collision domain → collision-free full-duplex links; multiple simultaneous conversations.
  • Still one broadcast domain (broadcast frames flood all ports).

Router

A network-layer device connecting different networks. It examines the destination IP address, consults a routing/forwarding table, and forwards the packet toward the destination network. Each router interface has its own IP and MAC address.

  • Routers separate both collision domains and broadcast domains; they are the glue of internetworks.

§ 05TCP/IP Protocol Suite

The protocol suite the Internet actually runs on — a hierarchy of interactive modules, each providing a specific service. Forouzan treats it as five layers.

#LayerDuty (what it moves)Data unitKey protocols
5ApplicationProcess-to-process logic for user servicesMessageHTTP, FTP, SMTP, DNS, TELNET, SSH, SNMP
4TransportEnd-to-end delivery between processes (ports)Segment / datagramTCP, UDP, SCTP
3NetworkHost-to-host delivery across many networks; routingDatagram (packet)IP, ICMP, IGMP, ARP*, routing protocols
2Data linkNode-to-node (hop) delivery across one link; framing, error detection, MACFrameEthernet, Wi-Fi, PPP
1PhysicalIndividual bits as electrical/optical/radio signalsBitLine coding, media specs

*ARP sits at the boundary of network and data-link layers; Forouzan discusses it with link-layer addressing.

Key ideas

  • Encapsulation: each layer wraps the unit from the layer above with its own header (data link also adds a trailer). Decapsulation reverses this at the destination.
  • Addressing per layer: application → names (URLs, email), transport → port numbers, network → logical/IP addresses, data link → link-layer/MAC addresses.
  • Scope: physical & data-link operate hop-by-hop; network, transport, application are end-to-end. Routers implement only layers 1–3; switches 1–2; hosts all five.
  • Multiplexing/demultiplexing: a layer can carry several higher-layer protocols (e.g., IP's protocol field, TCP/UDP port numbers).

§ 06The OSI Model

ISO's Open Systems Interconnection reference model (late 1970s): a seven-layer framework meant to let any two systems communicate regardless of underlying architecture. It is a model, not a protocol suite.

#LayerResponsibilities (memorise these)
7ApplicationNetwork services to the user: file transfer/access, mail, directory services, remote log-in.
6PresentationSyntax & semantics of exchanged data: translation (encoding formats), encryption, compression.
5SessionDialog control (who talks, half/full duplex) and synchronization (checkpoints in long transfers).
4TransportProcess-to-process delivery: service-point (port) addressing, segmentation & reassembly, connection control, end-to-end flow & error control.
3NetworkSource-to-destination delivery across networks: logical addressing, routing.
2Data linkHop-to-hop delivery: framing, physical (MAC) addressing, flow control, error control, medium access control.
1PhysicalBits over the medium: signal & interface characteristics, bit rate, synchronization, line configuration, topology, transmission mode.

Mnemonic (bottom-up): "Please Do Not Throw Sausage Pizza Away."

  • Peer-to-peer processes: layer x on the sender logically communicates with layer x on the receiver, using the layers below as services.
  • Interfaces between adjacent layers define the services exchanged, letting a layer's implementation change without breaking others (modularity).

Why OSI never replaced TCP/IP

  1. Timing: OSI arrived when TCP/IP was already deployed and heavily invested in — switching cost was too high.
  2. Incomplete/complex: some layers (session, presentation) were never fully specified or clearly needed; the model was seen as complicated.
  3. No high-quality implementations: early OSI software could not match TCP/IP's free, proven implementations, so it never showed enough advantage to justify migration.

§ 08Digital Signals

Information can be sent as a digital signal: data represented as a sequence of discrete voltage levels. With L levels, each level carries log₂ L bits (2 levels → 1 bit, 4 levels → 2 bits, 8 levels → 3 bits…).

Formulas
bits per level = log₂ L

Bit rate = bits transmitted per second (bps)

Bit length = propagation speed × bit duration

Bit length is the "distance one bit occupies on the wire" — the digital analogue of wavelength.

Worked example A signal has 8 levels and a baud-equivalent of 1 million signal elements/s. Bits per level = log₂8 = 3, so bit rate = 3 Mbps. Conversely, a page of 24 lines × 80 characters (8 bits each) downloaded 100 pages/s needs 24×80×8×100 = 1.536 Mbps.

Digital signal as composite analog

By Fourier analysis, a digital signal (with its vertical edges) is a composite analog signal with infinite bandwidth. Real channels have finite bandwidth, so edges are rounded — this is why bandwidth limits data rate (§10).

Transmission of digital signals

  • Baseband transmission — send the digital signal as-is, without modulation, over a low-pass channel (bandwidth starting from 0). Exclusive-use media (dedicated Ethernet cable) work this way. Needs bandwidth proportional to bit rate.
  • Broadband transmission — modulate the digital data onto an analog carrier to fit a band-pass channel (bandwidth not starting at 0), e.g., modems over telephone lines, cable TV data.

§ 09Transmission Impairment

Signals travelling through any medium suffer impairment: what is received is not exactly what was sent. Three causes:

1 · Attenuation

Loss of energy as the signal overcomes medium resistance (some energy becomes heat). Compensated with amplifiers (analog) or repeaters (digital). Measured in decibels:

Formula
dB = 10 · log₁₀ (P₂ / P₁)

Negative dB = loss, positive = gain. Decibels add along a path — that's why engineers use them.

Worked example Signal power halves: dB = 10·log₁₀(0.5) ≈ −3 dB. A link with segments of −3 dB, +7 dB (amplifier), −3 dB has net −3+7−3 = +1 dB.

2 · Distortion

Change in the signal's shape. In a composite signal, each frequency component travels at a slightly different speed, so components arrive with different phase shifts and the sum differs from the original.

3 · Noise

  • Thermal noise — random electron motion in the wire; unavoidable.
  • Induced noise — from external sources like motors and appliances (medium acts as receiving antenna).
  • Crosstalk — one wire's signal induced onto a neighbouring wire.
  • Impulse noise — short high-energy spikes (lightning, power lines).
Signal-to-Noise Ratio
SNR = avg. signal power / avg. noise power

SNRdB = 10 · log₁₀ (SNR)

e.g., signal 10 mW, noise 1 μW → SNR = 10000, SNRdB = 40 dB.

§ 10Data Rate Limits

The maximum data rate of a channel depends on three factors: available bandwidth, number of signal levels, and channel quality (noise). Two classical results:

Nyquist bit rate — noiseless channel

Formula
BitRate = 2 × B × log₂ L

B = bandwidth (Hz), L = number of signal levels. In theory L can grow without bound; in practice many levels are hard for the receiver to distinguish reliably — the formula gives an upper bound for a noiseless channel.

Shannon capacity — noisy channel

Formula
C = B × log₂ (1 + SNR)

C is the theoretical highest rate at any error-free coding; note it has no "levels" term — noise, not levels, is the binding constraint.

Worked examples (exam favourites)

1. Telephone line: B = 3000 Hz, SNR = 3162. C = 3000 × log₂(3163) ≈ 3000 × 11.62 ≈ 34.86 kbps.

2. Extremely noisy channel: SNR ≈ 0 → C = B·log₂(1) = 0. No capacity regardless of bandwidth.

3. Using both: B = 1 MHz, SNR = 63. Shannon: C = 10⁶·log₂64 = 6 Mbps. Choose a practical target, say 4 Mbps, then Nyquist gives levels: 4×10⁶ = 2×10⁶×log₂L → log₂L = 2 → L = 4.

Method to remember: Shannon gives the ceiling; Nyquist tells you how many levels you need to approach your chosen rate.

§ 11Performance

Bandwidth — two meanings

  • Bandwidth in hertz: range of frequencies a channel passes (e.g., telephone line ≈ 4 kHz).
  • Bandwidth in bps: number of bits a link can transmit per second (e.g., Fast Ethernet = 100 Mbps). The two are related (§10) but not interchangeable — state units in answers.

Throughput

How fast data actually passes through a point — always ≤ bandwidth (the link may be rated 1 Mbps but deliver 200 kbps under load). Bandwidth is the potential; throughput is the measurement.

Latency (delay)

Formula
Latency = propagation + transmission + queuing + processing

Propagation time = distance / propagation speed

Transmission time = message size / bandwidth
Worked example 2.5 kB message, 12000 km link, speed 2.4×10⁸ m/s, bandwidth 1 Gbps.
Propagation = 1.2×10⁷ / 2.4×10⁸ = 50 ms. Transmission = 20000 bits / 10⁹ bps = 0.02 ms. Dominated by propagation → for short messages, distance matters more than bandwidth. For a 5 MB file on the same link, transmission = 40 ms — now comparable.

Bandwidth–delay product

BDP = bandwidth × round-trip (or one-way) delay = the number of bits that "fill the pipe" — the volume of the link. A sender should be able to have this many bits outstanding to use the link fully (motivates sliding windows later in the course). E.g., 1 Mbps × 5 ms = 5000 bits.

Jitter

Variation in delay between packets of the same flow. Critical for audio/video: if packet delays are 20 ms, 45 ms, 20 ms, playback becomes uneven even though average delay is fine.

§ 12Line Coding — Unipolar & Polar

Line coding converts a sequence of bits into a digital signal. The receiver recovers the bits by sampling the levels. Syllabus scope: unipolar and polar schemes only.

Design issues to judge every scheme by

  • Baseline wandering — receiver tracks a running average (baseline) of signal power; long runs of the same level drift it and cause misreads.
  • DC component — constant (zero-frequency) energy; cannot pass through transformers/AC-coupled systems.
  • Self-synchronization — transitions in the signal let the receiver keep its clock aligned; long flat stretches lose sync. (A clock fast by 0.1% misreads a long run badly.)
  • Complexity / bandwidth cost — more transitions ⇒ higher signal rate ⇒ more bandwidth.

§ 13Transmission Media — Guided

Guided media provide a physical conduit from device to device.

Twisted-pair cable

  • Two insulated copper conductors twisted together; one carries signal, the other is ground reference — the receiver uses the difference.
  • Why twist? Interference and crosstalk affect both wires nearly equally when twisted, so unwanted signals cancel in the difference. More twists/metre → better quality.
  • UTP (unshielded) vs STP (foil shield around each pair — less crosstalk, bulkier, costlier).
  • Categories: Cat 3 (voice+data, 10 Mbps) … Cat 5/5e (100 Mbps–1 Gbps) … Cat 6 (up to 10 Gbps, short runs). Connector: RJ-45.
  • Uses: telephone local loop, DSL, LANs (10Base-T, 100Base-T).

Coaxial cable

  • Central core conductor + insulating sheath + outer braided/foil conductor (shield & second conductor) + jacket. Carries higher-frequency signals than twisted pair.
  • Rated by RG numbers: RG-59 (cable TV, 75 Ω), RG-58 (thin Ethernet, 50 Ω), RG-11 (thick Ethernet). Connector: BNC.
  • Higher bandwidth than TP but attenuates faster — needs frequent repeaters. Uses: cable TV, traditional Ethernet, cable modems.

Fiber-optic cable

  • Glass/plastic core in a cladding of lower refractive index; transmits light by total internal reflection (angle above the critical angle).
  • Propagation modes:
    • Multimode step-index — many rays, abrupt core/cladding density change → path-length spread → distortion.
    • Multimode graded-index — density decreases from centre outward, bending rays → less distortion.
    • Single-mode — tiny core and focused light, essentially one horizontal ray → lowest distortion, longest distances.
  • Advantages: huge bandwidth, low attenuation (repeaters every ~50 km vs ~5 km for coax), immune to electromagnetic noise, light weight, hard to tap.
  • Disadvantages: installation/maintenance expertise, unidirectional light (two fibers for duplex), cost.

§ 14Switching — Introduction & Packet Switching

A large network cannot connect every pair of hosts directly (mesh) or through one giant hub. Instead, interlinked switches create temporary paths. Three methods exist — circuit switching, packet switching, message switching (obsolete); the Internet uses packet switching, where data is chunked into packets forwarded switch-to-switch.

Datagram approach (connectionless)

  • Each packet (datagram) is independent, even within one message; no setup/teardown; the network keeps no connection state.
  • Each switch/router has a forwarding table based on destination address; because routes can change, packets may take different paths, arrive out of order, or be dropped (best-effort). Reordering/recovery is left to higher layers.
  • The destination address stays in the packet end-to-end. Used at the Internet's network layer (IP).

Virtual-circuit approach (connection-oriented)

  • Three phases: setup (request + acknowledgement create table entries in every switch on the path), data transfer, teardown.
  • Packets carry a short VCI (virtual-circuit identifier) with local link scope — each switch swaps incoming VCI for outgoing VCI using its table, so full addresses are only needed during setup.
  • All packets of a flow follow the same path → in-order arrival; resources can be allocated at setup (better QoS) or on demand.
  • Historically implemented in WANs like Frame Relay and ATM; conceptually a hybrid of circuit switching (path setup) and datagram switching (still packets, still store-and-forward).

§ 15Data-Link Layer — Introduction

The Internet is a combination of networks glued by connecting devices. Communication is a series of nodes (hosts and routers) joined by links (wired/wireless networks). The data-link layer is responsible for moving a datagram across one link — hop-to-hop delivery.

Link types

  • Point-to-point link — medium dedicated to two devices.
  • Broadcast link — medium shared by several devices (classic Ethernet, Wi-Fi) → needs medium access control.

Two sublayers

  • DLC (data link control) — duties common to all links: framing, flow control, error control.
  • MAC (media access control) — only for broadcast links: who may transmit when (§19).

Services offered

  • Framing — encapsulate the datagram into a frame with header (and trailer) so bits are grouped and addressed per hop.
  • Flow control — keep a fast sender from overwhelming a slow receiver's buffers.
  • Error control — detect (and sometimes correct) errors introduced by the physical layer; corrupted frames are discarded or retransmitted.
  • Congestion control — rarely at this layer; usually an end-to-end issue.

Each link may run a different link-layer protocol (Ethernet, Wi-Fi, PPP), so a datagram may be re-framed at every hop while the datagram itself stays intact end-to-end.

§ 16Link-Layer Addressing & ARP

Why another address?

IP addresses identify source and destination end-to-end; they cannot tell you which device is next on the current link (and a datagram is passed through routers whose links change). Each hop is a separate "postal leg" needing its own link-layer (MAC) address.

  • Ethernet MAC: 48 bits, written as six colon-separated hex bytes, e.g. 4A:30:10:21:10:1A; burned into the NIC.
  • Unicast (one recipient), multicast (group), broadcast = FF:FF:FF:FF:FF:FF (all hosts on the link). In Ethernet, a multicast/broadcast address has the least-significant bit of the first byte = 1.
  • In each frame: destination MAC = next hop, source MAC = this hop's sender. MAC addresses change at every hop; IP addresses in the datagram do not.

ARP — Address Resolution Protocol

DefinitionARP maps a known IP address of the next hop to its MAC address on the local link.
  1. Sender knows next-hop IP (its own routing decision) but not the MAC.
  2. It broadcasts an ARP request: "Who has IP x? Tell me your MAC." (Destination MAC = broadcast, because the target is unknown.)
  3. Every host on the link receives it; only the owner of IP x replies, and the ARP reply is unicast back (the requester's MAC was in the request).
  4. The mapping is stored in the ARP cache for a while so the exchange isn't repeated per packet.

ARP packet essentials

  • Fields: hardware type (1 = Ethernet), protocol type (0x0800 = IPv4), hardware & protocol address lengths (6, 4), operation (1 = request, 2 = reply), then sender/target hardware & protocol addresses. In a request, the target hardware address field is all 0s (unknown).
  • Carried directly in an Ethernet frame (type 0x0806).
Exam pointerStandard question: host A (with IP/MAC given) sends to host B via router R — show the ARP exchanges and the MAC/IP addresses in each frame on each link. Key: IP addresses = A and B throughout; MACs on link 1 = A→R, on link 2 = R→B. ARP resolves the next hop's IP, which is B only on the final link.

§ 17Error Detection & Correction — Block Coding

Types of errors

  • Single-bit error — exactly one bit flips.
  • Burst error — two or more bits flipped; burst length = distance from first to last corrupted bit (bits inside need not all be corrupted). More likely in practice because noise lasts longer than one bit time.

The central idea of all schemes: redundancy — extra bits computed from the data, sent along, and re-checked by the receiver. Detection asks only "did an error occur?"; correction must also locate the flipped bits (much harder).

Block coding

  • Divide the message into datawords of k bits; add r redundant bits to make codewords of n = k + r bits.
  • Only 2k of the 2n possible words are valid codewords. Receiver: if the received word is not valid → error detected. (If corruption turns one valid codeword into another valid one, the error slips through.)

Hamming distance

Key results
d(x, y) = number of differing bit positions = weight of x ⊕ y

Detect up to s errors ⇔ dmin ≥ s + 1

Correct up to t errors ⇔ dmin ≥ 2t + 1

dmin = smallest Hamming distance between any two valid codewords of the code.

Parity-check code (the simplest linear block code)

  • n = k + 1: append one parity bit making the total number of 1s even (even parity).
  • Receiver computes the syndrome = parity of all n received bits; syndrome 1 → discard.
  • dmin = 2 ⇒ detects all odd numbers of bit errors (1, 3, 5…); an even number of flips goes undetected. Cannot correct anything.
Worked exampleDataword 1011, even parity: it has three 1s (odd), so parity bit = 1 → codeword 10111 (four 1s, even). One flip: received 10011 has three 1s → odd → syndrome 1 → error detected. Two flips: received 01011 (bits 1 and 2 flipped) has… still an even count of changes, e.g. 00110 from flipping bits 1 and 5 gives two 1s → even → syndrome 0 → error undetected. Any even number of flips escapes parity.

§ 18Cyclic Redundancy Check (CRC)

A cyclic code: any circular shift of a valid codeword is another valid codeword. CRC is the cyclic code used in Ethernet, Wi-Fi and most link layers — implemented with cheap shift registers and XOR gates.

Encoding procedure

  1. Choose a divisor (generator) of r + 1 bits (equivalently, a generator polynomial of degree r).
  2. Append r zeros to the k-bit dataword.
  3. Divide by the generator using binary (modulo-2) division — subtraction is XOR, no borrows.
  4. The r-bit remainder is the CRC; replace the appended zeros with it. Codeword = dataword + CRC.

Decoding

Receiver divides the whole received codeword by the same generator. Remainder (syndrome) = 0 → accept (data = first k bits); non-zero → corrupted, discard.

Worked example (Forouzan classic)

Dataword = 1001 (k = 4), generator = 1011 (r = 3). Augmented dataword: 1001000.

  1001000 ÷ 1011 (XOR division)
  1001000
⊕ 1011        → 0010000
    10000
  ⊕ 1011      → 00110
  remainder = 110

CRC = 110 → codeword = 1001110. Receiver: divide 1001110 by 1011 → remainder 000 → accepted.

Polynomial view

A bit pattern maps to a polynomial: 1011 ↔ x³ + x + 1. Generators are quoted as polynomials, e.g. Ethernet's CRC-32. Degree of generator = number of CRC bits r.

What a good generator catches

  • Generator with ≥ 2 terms (i.e., x⁰ coefficient 1 and more) detects all single-bit errors.
  • A generator with factor (x + 1) detects all odd numbers of errors.
  • Detects all burst errors of length ≤ r; bursts of length r + 1 slip through with probability ≈ 1/2r−1, longer bursts ≈ 1/2r.

§ 19Media Access Control: CSMA & CSMA/CD

On a broadcast link, if two nodes transmit at once the frames collide and are destroyed. Random-access protocols coordinate without a master: each station decides for itself when to send.

CSMA — Carrier Sense Multiple Access

Principle: "listen before talk" — sense the medium; transmit only if idle. Collisions still happen because of propagation delay: a station may sense "idle" while another's signal hasn't reached it yet.

Vulnerable time
Vulnerable time (CSMA) = Tp (max propagation time)

A frame sent at t is at risk from any station that senses the medium before the first bit arrives at it.

Persistence methods (what to do when the line is busy/idle)

  • 1-persistent: if idle, send immediately (probability 1). Simple; highest collision chance — all waiting stations fire the moment the line frees. Used by Ethernet.
  • Non-persistent: if busy, wait a random time, then sense again. Fewer collisions, but wastes idle time.
  • p-persistent (for slotted channels): if idle, transmit with probability p; with probability 1−p wait for the next slot and repeat. Compromise between the two.

CSMA/CD — with Collision Detection

Adds: keep listening while transmitting. If the received energy differs from what you sent (energy level indicates collision), abort immediately, send a brief jam signal, and retry after a random binary-exponential backoff (wait a random multiple of slot time from 0…2K−1, K = min(attempts, 10); give up after 15 attempts). Aborting early saves the bandwidth that would be wasted finishing a doomed frame.

Minimum frame size condition
Tfr ≥ 2 × Tp ⇔ frame size ≥ 2 × Tp × bandwidth

Worst case: the collision happens just before your first bit reaches the far end (Tp), and the collision signal takes Tp to come back. You must still be transmitting when it returns, or you'll never know your frame died.

Worked example (memorise) Classic Ethernet: 10 Mbps, maximum Tp = 25.6 μs. Minimum frame time = 2 × 25.6 = 51.2 μs → minimum frame = 10⁷ × 51.2×10⁻⁶ = 512 bits = 64 bytes. This is exactly Ethernet's minimum frame size.

CSMA/CD flow: sense (persistence method) → transmit while monitoring → if Tfr elapses with no collision, success → if collision, jam + backoff + retry.

  3. Build the connection-oriented vs connectionless comparison table.
  4. Place hub/switch/router on their layers and explain collision vs broadcast domains.
  5. Name all 7 OSI layers with two functions each, and all 5 TCP/IP layers with their data units and 3 protocols each.
  6. Give 3 reasons OSI didn't replace TCP/IP.
  7. Compute dB loss/gain, SNRdB, Nyquist rate, Shannon capacity — and combine Shannon + Nyquist in one problem.
  8. Compute propagation time, transmission time, and bandwidth–delay product; define throughput vs bandwidth and jitter.
  9. Draw unipolar NRZ, NRZ-L, NRZ-I, RZ, Manchester, Differential Manchester for any 8-bit stream, and rank them on DC component / sync / bandwidth.
  10. Describe twisted pair (and why twisting helps), coax, and the 3 fiber propagation modes.
  11. Compare datagram vs virtual-circuit packet switching (phases, VCI, ordering, state).
  12. State the DLL's services and its two sublayers.
  13. Walk through ARP request/reply for a host→router→host path, with correct MAC/IP in every frame.
  14. Use dmin rules (s+1, 2t+1); do a parity example and explain what it misses.
  15. Perform CRC encoding & checking for dataword 1001, generator 1011 — by hand.
  16. Explain the 3 CSMA persistence methods, CSMA vulnerable time, and derive Ethernet's 64-byte minimum frame from Tfr ≥ 2Tp.

If every item is a yes, Unit I is done. Weakest spots for most students: line-coding drawings, the ARP frame-addressing question, and CRC by hand — practise those three on paper.
